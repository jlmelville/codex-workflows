# Research Depth

Improve the investigation's ability to answer the user's question. More sources, disciplines, tools,
or output are useful only when they resolve a consequential uncertainty.

## Fix The Objective

Identify the decision or understanding the user wants, the deliverable, and the constraints to
preserve. Distinguish exploring possibilities from testing a claim. Make consequential ambiguities
explicit; resolve what available context can settle before asking the user.

Deepening the method does not authorize changing the objective, expanding implementation, or
starting external actions. Surface a worthwhile broader question with its payoff and cost when it
exceeds the request, while continuing useful work inside scope.

## Learn The Expert Approach

Name what a strong practitioner would do that the request leaves out: a different problem framing,
assumption to test, source of evidence, or method. Describe the action and its purpose rather than
adopting an expert persona.

When the relevant practice is unfamiliar, disputed, or potentially stale, investigate it before
settling the approach. Find authoritative work relevant to this exact question: original research,
standards, documented practice, reference implementations, or inspectable successful artifacts.
Choose sources for demonstrated relevance and methods rather than reputation alone. Use reviews to
orient the search, then inspect the primary work behind consequential claims, including methods and
limitations. Read enough to explain how the practice would change this investigation; abstracts,
search snippets, and lists of famous experts are insufficient.

Tie each important methodological choice to inspected evidence or label it as a proposed approach.
Look for credible disagreement and the conditions that explain it. Several sources repeating one
underlying claim do not provide independent support. Bound further reading by the unresolved
decision; do not promise to digest an entire field before making progress.

## Make The Research Design Concrete

Present a compact brief that connects these decisions:

- **Overlooked expert moves:** what changes in the investigation and why it could change the answer.
- **Primary material:** the closest useful evidence, such as source code at a named revision, raw
  measurements, event records, original papers and supplements, or first-hand observations. Identify
  what is available, how derived inputs were produced, and what is missing. Rawness alone does not
  establish quality; validated data may be the appropriate starting point.
- **Methods and tools:** name the analysis, experiment, comparison, or derivation and the tools that
  can perform it here. Connect them to a question, assumptions, and likely failure modes. Compare
  plausible approaches before committing; prefer the simplest one that can distinguish the answers.
- **Outside evidence:** specify what observable result would support, weaken, or refute the claim,
  how it will be obtained, and what it cannot establish. Choose evidence that could expose a
  plausible wrong answer. Model agreement, polished prose, and a self-assigned quality score are
  insufficient.

Bring in another field when its methods address a named blind spot. Explain the transfer and its
limits; assembling fictional specialists or adding disciplines without a purpose does not add depth.
For ambiguity about methods or evidence, consult
[methods-and-evidence.md](references/methods-and-evidence.md).

## Rewrite And Follow Through

Rewrite the task as a self-contained prompt using the brief. Preserve the user's goal and
constraints; include the selected material, methods, consequential assumptions, validation, stopping
condition, and expected result. State missing access or evidence explicitly. Use concrete actions in
place of demands for exceptional quality, unlimited tokens, or certainty.

If the user asked for a prompt or research design, deliver that artifact. If they asked to perform
the investigation, use the strengthened formulation to continue the authorized work. Prompt
improvement is an intermediate step in that case, not the completion condition.

Start with a small observation or experiment that can expose a mistaken premise before committing to
an elaborate method. Keep exploratory findings distinct from confirmation: reserve fresh data or a
separate check when a discovery is to be presented as validated. Preserve enough source, version,
transformation, and command information to inspect or reproduce consequential results.

Report findings with their supporting sources or artifacts, meaningful contrary evidence, and the
limits of the checks actually performed. A reproducible calculation can still test the wrong model;
a successful structural check does not establish real-world effectiveness. Where no decisive test
exists, use converging evidence and calibrated uncertainty rather than inventing a binary verdict.

Stop when the evidence supports the requested decision at the needed confidence, or when further
progress depends on unavailable material or a new scope decision. Identify the specific remaining
uncertainty and the observation that would resolve it; do not expand the work merely to keep going.
